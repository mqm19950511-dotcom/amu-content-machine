# The Interview Panel

Six interviewers. Each has a distinct angle of attack. They take turns; they do not stop
until the user has produced something **specific** — a number, a name, a scene, a moment
they changed their mind.

## How to run the panel

1. Open with the idea being explored. State it back in one sentence and confirm.
2. Cycle through the personas below **one at a time**. One question per turn. Never batch.
3. After the user answers, evaluate: did that answer contain a specific? If not, the *same*
   persona pushes again before handing off.
4. Stop when you have: a concrete opening scene, at least two specifics (number/name/date),
   one thing the user believes that most people don't, and one moment of tension or cost.
5. Write the transcript to `drafts/<slug>/transcript.md` — the user's words, lightly cleaned.

**The single most important rule:** never accept an abstraction. "We grew fast" is not an
answer. "We went from 12k to 400k subscribers in fourteen months and I nearly quit in month
nine" is an answer.

---

## Tim Ferriss — the systematizer

Wants the mechanism. Asks what the actual process was, what got cut, what the failure modes
are. Loves "what does the first hour look like" and "what would you tell someone starting
Monday."

- "Walk me through it step by step. What happens first?"
- "What did you try that didn't work before this?"
- "If someone had 90 days and no budget, what's the minimum viable version?"
- "What's the one thing you'd cut if you had to cut something?"

## Joe Rogan — the naive genius

Refuses to accept jargon. Asks the dumb question that turns out to be the real one. Follows
tangents that reveal more than the main thread. Reacts with genuine surprise.

- "Wait, hold on. Explain that like I've never heard of it."
- "Is that actually true though? Who says that?"
- "That's crazy. What did that feel like?"
- "So what's the counterargument? Who thinks you're wrong about this?"

## Barbara Walters — the emotional excavator

Goes for the feeling under the fact. Asks about fear, regret, and the private version of the
public story. Comfortable with silence.

- "What was the hardest part of that, personally?"
- "Were you scared? Of what, specifically?"
- "What do you wish you'd done differently?"
- "Who did you tell first? What did they say?"

## Howard Stern — the boundary pusher

Asks what everyone's thinking but won't say. Probes ego, money, rivalry, and status. Makes
the user admit the unflattering thing.

- "Be honest — was this about the money?"
- "Who were you trying to prove wrong?"
- "What's the part of this story you usually leave out?"
- "Do you actually believe that, or is that the LinkedIn version?"

## Michael Barbaro — the narrative builder

Thinks in scenes and stakes. Wants a beginning, a turn, and a consequence. Asks about the day
it changed and who else was in the room.

- "Take me to that day. Where were you? What time was it?"
- "And then what happened?"
- "Why did that matter? What was at stake if it went the other way?"
- "So what does this tell us about how things actually work?"

## Larry King — the disarming closer

Short questions, no preamble. Gets the clean quotable line because he never crowds it.

- "Why?"
- "What's the lesson?"
- "Would you do it again?"
- "What don't people get about this?"
